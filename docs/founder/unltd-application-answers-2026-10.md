## About you
I'm Francis Cobbinah, a software architect. For over 20 years I worked as a contractor on the Microsoft stack, and my career went well. In 2023 I was struck down by COVID, pneumonia, diabetes and kidney failure, all at the same time. I spent three months in intensive care and more than two years stepping back to recover. I now attend dialysis three times a week while I wait for a life-changing operation.

I have also faced homelessness, so I know what it is to walk into an interview underprepared, when the job is the difference between everything and nothing. And I know how hard it is to find your confidence again after a long illness.

Interviews are one of the biggest barriers for people without coaching, contacts or confidence: people returning to work after illness, college and university leavers, and anyone with interview anxiety. Good practice is expensive or embarrassing, so the people who need it most often go without.

Six months ago I decided to use my time in the dialysis chair to build something that would last. TheInterviewChair.com lets anyone practice a real interview with live AI interviewers who talk back, in 32 languages, and get honest feedback on what to improve. It is free to try with no account, private by default, and needs no camera. The idea is simple: make your mistakes here, not in the real interview.

I built it alone, between treatments, and I am launching this week. My own experience is why I will keep it accessible: I want that first step back into work to feel safer for everyone.

## What is the social or environmental issue you are addressing?
An interview is the doorway to work, and for many people it is the hardest door to get through. The people who struggle most are not short of ability. They are short of practice, confidence and someone to practice with. This affects people returning to work after a long illness, college and university leavers entering a very difficult time, people with low confidence, and people who freeze or panic with interview anxiety.

The scale is large. In May to July 2026, 20.9% of working-age people in the UK were economically inactive (ONS). The employment rate for disabled people is 52.8%, against 82.5% for non-disabled people (DWP, Q2 2025), and 21.7% of economically inactive disabled people say they want to work (DWP, 2024/25). Among the young, 1.012 million 16 to 24 year olds were not in education, employment or training in January to March 2026, the first time above one million since 2013 (ONS). **(update if newer releases are out)**

Good interview practice is hard to find. Coaching is expensive, friends and family are not always available, and practising in front of another person can feel embarrassing. So many people go into the real interview having never said their answers out loud, and capable people lose opportunities and confidence with every rejection. A long gap from work makes that worse, because the gap itself becomes something to explain.

I know this from experience. I have faced homelessness and walked into an interview underprepared when the job meant everything. I am now rebuilding my working life after serious illness, while on dialysis three times a week. Confidence is the first thing a long illness takes, and the hardest thing to get back.

## How do you plan to tackle this social or environmental issue?
I have built TheInterviewChair.com, a place to practice real interviews privately, with live AI interviewers who talk back, in 32 languages. Anyone can try it free with no account and no camera. People get an honest score on five areas, see where they were weak and a stronger answer, and can go again. The idea is simple: make your mistakes here, not in the real interview.

It is designed for the people above. Everything is private by default and nothing is shared with anyone unless the person chooses. People can pause, repeat or pass any question, so it never feels like a test. A new mode, Day One Ready, prepares people for their first 90 days in a new job, which matters most to anyone coming back after a break.

The platform is built and launching this week. It had more than a thousand visits to its pages before any advertising. The grant would let me reach the people it is for, in three ways: (1) free full interviews for people who cannot afford them, offered through partners such as university careers services, colleges and organisations that support people back into work after illness; (2) more support for interview anxiety, such as gentler pacing and confidence-building exercises; (3) a simple way to measure impact, building on the confidence question we already ask, by tracking how people feel before and after they practice and how their scores improve when they go again.

I am building this alone, so the grant would turn a working product into one that reaches the people who need it most.

## How I arrived at the figures
How I counted "benefit directly": a person counts only if they actually completed something, such as a practice interview, a CV analysis or a Day One Ready session. A visit to the website does not count. I take the numbers from the platform's own usage records and count different people, not repeat sessions.

How I reached 500 for the next 12 months: these are assumptions, and I have set them low on purpose. The site had more than a thousand visits in its first few weeks with no advertising, so I assume around 10,000 visits over the year as I launch publicly, keep sharing it, and work with partners. I assume about 5% of visitors will complete a practice, which is cautious because the free demo takes only about three minutes and needs no account. That gives about 500 people.

I will check these assumptions against real figures every month and report what actually happens. If completion is higher than 5%, or reach grows faster through partners, the number will be higher. I would rather tell you a figure I can beat than one I might miss.

## How do you ensure that your work is accessible and inclusive?
The product is built to be as easy and as safe to use as possible, for people who may already feel anxious or out of practice.

Free to try with no account, so there is no barrier to a first go. It works on phones and computers. Everything is private by default, and there is no camera, so nobody has to be seen. People can answer out loud or type, and every question is shown on screen as well as spoken. They can pause, repeat or pass any question, and go again as many times as they like. The interviewers are available in 32 languages, so people can practice in the language they will really use. The help pages are written in plain English.

The tone is deliberate: the message is "make your mistakes here, not in the real interview", so nobody is judged. A mode called Day One Ready supports people starting a new job after a break. The price for ongoing use is low, and I plan to offer free full interviews through partner organisations for people who cannot afford them.

I am building this from lived experience of long-term illness, which shapes the design. I also know there is more to do. I plan to run an accessibility review with people who use screen readers and keyboard navigation, improve text size and contrast options, and listen to feedback from the groups it is meant for, so it keeps getting easier for everyone.

## How do you generate income to keep your social venture going?
The venture earns its own income by selling the service, so it does not depend on grants to keep going.

People preparing for an interview can try it free, with no account, and their first full interview is free. After that they pay £4.99 a month, or a one-off £5.99 pass for someone with a single interview coming up (up to 10 practice sessions over 7 days).

Recruiters and employers pay for the platform: recruiters pay per seat each month and a small fee each time they send a candidate a preparation link, and employers pay a monthly platform fee. Their payments help me keep practice affordable for the individuals it is built for. People can also buy a practice interview as a gift for someone else.

The prices are based on measured costs. A full practice interview with live AI interviewers costs me roughly 44 pence to run, so the prices cover the cost with a margin, and I know exactly how much a free interview costs me. That is how I can offer free access responsibly.

My plan is to use part of the income, and any grant, to give free full interviews to people who cannot afford to pay, through partner organisations. I am self-funded and have taken no outside investment. The payment system is set up and live, and the amounts so far are small because I am only now launching publicly.

## Over the next 12 months, how do you plan to grow your social venture?
The next 12 months have three stages.

Months 1 to 3, launch and learn. TheInterviewChair.com launches publicly on Product Hunt this month. I will share it through my LinkedIn network of over 4,700 followers, short videos and regular posts, and I am documenting the build openly in a founder's diary so people can follow along. I will watch the numbers closely, fix whatever stops people finishing a practice, and keep making it feel safe and friendly.

Months 4 to 6, partnerships. I will approach university and college careers services, organisations that help people back into work after illness, and community groups, and offer them a small free pilot. Their members would get free full interviews, and in return I would learn what works for those groups. I will start with a handful of partners and do it properly, not sign up many and do none well.

Months 7 to 12, grow and measure. I will expand what has worked, extend the languages and the home-page content beyond English, and add more support for interview anxiety and for people starting a new job. I will track how people feel before and after practising and how their scores improve, and use that evidence to show the impact and to go back to partners and funders.

Income from paying customers, including recruiters and employers, keeps the venture running and pays for the free places. A grant would speed this up, funding the free interviews for people who cannot afford them, the partner pilots and an accessibility review.

I am building this alone, so I will grow at a pace I can sustain and do well, rather than chase the biggest number.
